# 🧠 Is It Time to Rethink the Name “ADHD”?

> ADHD stands for Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder — but the name doesn’t tell the whole story.

---

## 🔍 Quick Summary

- “Attention deficit” is misleading; the real challenge is regulating attention.
- “Hyperactivity” isn’t always bouncing-off-the-walls energy; it can be racing thoughts or restlessness.
- ADHD used to be called ADD — the name changed in 1987, and that change wasn’t perfect.
- The words “deficit” and “disorder” can feed stigma and shame.
- Experts suggest reframing it: Neurodiversity, VAST, or Disorder of Regulation.

---

## What ADHD Really Means

ADHD is a neurodevelopmental condition. The brain works differently from a neurotypical brain, and those differences are often inherited (Hallowell & Ratey, ADHD 2.0).

There are three presentations in the DSM-5:

- Predominantly Inattentive – focus drifts, time slips, organization is a challenge, and forgetfulness is common. This was once called “ADD” (Solden, Women with Attention Deficit Disorder).
- Predominantly Hyperactive-Impulsive – restlessness, quick decisions, and difficulty slowing down.
- Combined – a mix of inattentive and hyperactive/impulsive traits.

---

## From “ADD” to “ADHD”

If you were diagnosed before the late 80s, you probably heard ADD. That’s because in 1980, the DSM-III officially listed Attention Deficit Disorder, with two types: “with hyperactivity” and “without hyperactivity.”

In 1987, the DSM-III-R merged both into a single name: Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder — even if hyperactivity wasn’t part of your experience. The intention was good: research showed attention issues and hyperactivity often shared the same roots. But the new name had side effects:

- Inattentive folks felt overlooked
- “Hyperactivity” reinforced the stereotype of disruptive kids
- Many women and adults went undiagnosed because they didn’t “look” hyperactive

ADD is still used informally today, but it’s no longer an official diagnosis.

---

## Why the Name Falls Short

### “Attention Deficit” isn’t accurate

- The issue isn’t less attention
- Many with ADHD can hyperfocus for hours on something interesting (McCabe, How to ADHD)
- The struggle is directing and shifting attention when needed (Carder, ADHD: A Guide to Understanding)

### “Hyperactivity” is misunderstood

- In kids, it can mean running, fidgeting, climbing
- In adults, it’s often internal — racing thoughts, mental restlessness, rapid speech (Solden)
- Some ADHDers have little or no visible hyperactivity

### “Deficit” and “Disorder” can hurt self-worth

- They make ADHD sound like a flaw
- They push people to hide their diagnosis
- They feed the “lazy, broken” narrative (Paul, Order from Chaos; Brewis & Wutich, Lazy, Crazy, and Disgusting)

---

## How Experts Are Reframing It

- Neurodiversity – ADHD as a difference, not a defect (Kemp & Mitchelson)
- VAST (Variable Attention Stimulus Trait) – coined by Dr. Ned Hallowell to describe attention that shifts dramatically with interest and environment
- Disorder of Regulation – a broader view of ADHD that includes challenges with attention, emotions, time, and energy control (Carder)

---

## Why This Matters

Dr. Jaclyn Paul says it best: ADHD is a neurobiological disorder, not a character flaw. The name you use shapes the story you tell — to yourself and to others. If the label makes you feel defective or misunderstood, it can get in the way of self-acceptance, diagnosis, and support.

Knowing the history of the term, and why it doesn’t quite fit, can help you explain ADHD more clearly and challenge the old stereotypes that keep hanging on.
